Air can sort copper from aluminum.

Shredded lithium-ion batteries leave a mix of thin aluminum and copper foils. Blow air up a column at the right speed. The aluminum leaves at the top and the copper falls out at the bottom.

Where the separator works.

Each square is one case of the model, at one air flow and one pulse strength. The stronger the color of a square, the better that case sorts.

At low air flow the pulse helps. It frees the aluminum that steady air leaves stuck. At high air flow the pulse hurts. It carries copper out of the top.

The best cases sit between 70 and 85 m³/h with a pulse. Zhu and coworkers found 84 m³/h best for foils of this size.

Tap a square to run that case in the lab.

The separation efficiency is the share of the aluminum that leaves at the top plus the share of the copper that leaves at the bottom, minus 100%. It is 100% when every foil ends up in the right place.

It only works because they are foils.

Every particle has a hover speed. That is the air speed at which drag just carries its weight. Faster air lifts it. Slower air lets it sink.

For a solid ball, weight grows with volume but drag grows only with area. A bigger ball needs faster air.

For a foil, weight and drag both grow with its area, so the area cancels. Only thickness and density are left. A foil of 1.25 mm weighs 6.5 times less than a ball of the same size and shows the air about the same face.

The separator can only work where the air lifts aluminum but not copper. Move the sliders and see where that window lands.

The model against the real machine.

The points are measurements by Zhu and coworkers on a laboratory separator of this size. The thin lines are the two cases in our course notebook, steady air and air pulsed at 80%, with nothing fitted to these curves.

The thick line is computed in your browser, right now, for the pulse you choose. Drag the pulse strength. With a weak pulse the aluminum curve starts low, as measured, but the copper curve stays too high. With a strong pulse the copper curve falls, as measured, but all the aluminum is recovered. No single pulse setting matches both metals.

The model is also sharper than the machine at every setting. Its foils are identical. Real fragments differ in thickness and shape, and that is the next thing to add.

The model, and what it leaves out.

Each particle obeys Newton's second law with gravity, buoyancy and drag. The forces from accelerating air are about a thousand times smaller and are left out of the many-particle model. The air follows a prescribed turbulent profile. Particles collide with each other and with the wall, and they are counted when they reach an outlet.

The live columns on this page use the same equations as the notebook, stepped every 2 ms. They use fresh random turbulence in each run, so the numbers move by a few points from run to run.

What it assumes
  • The air flow rate is set by hand. The pulse frequency and strength of the real machine were not reported.
  • The foil drag law is calibrated on one suspension test with 0.1 mm foils of 5 mm.
  • Every foil has the same thickness and an ideal shape.
  • Particles do not slow the air down.
What it leaves out
  • Tumbling, bending and rotation of the foils.
  • A solved air flow. The profile is an assumed one, also in the cones.
  • Three dimensions. Collisions happen in one plane.
  • A continuous feed. Each run follows one batch for 20 s.
